SEB Poster – Research Overview

This research investigates how trait choice and microclimate variation impacts the thermal sensitivity and realised performance of the Harlequin ladybeetle, Harmonia axyridis. This research integrates laboratory thermal performance experiments with fine-scale environmental microclimate data to make robust, precise predictions of organismal performance and persistence.

Key Findings

  • Thermal sensitivity and realised performance differs among traits.
  • Microclimate variation alters realised performance.
  • Trait-specific TPCs may improve ecological predictions.

Methods

This research combines controlled laboratory experiments with high-resolution environmental data to investigate how temperature and microclimate influences insect performance under natural conditions.

Laboratory experiments

  • Adult Harmonia axyridis were reared under controlled laboratory conditions.
  • Performance was measured across a broad thermal gradient (5–48 °C).
  • Traits measured included:
    • Walking speed
    • Metabolic rate
    • Fecundity
    • Egg hatching success
  • Thermal performance curves (TPCs) were fitted using multiple biologically relevant models, with the best-supported model selected using AIC.

Microclimate measurements

  • Temperature loggers were deployed across multiple microhabitats around Stellenbosch, South Africa.
  • Temperatures were recorded at fine (15 minute) temporal resolution over a two-year period. However, the results for this SEB poster only uses the summer data - December to March.
  • These datasets were used to characterise the thermal environments experienced by free-living ladybeetles.

Data integration

  • Laboratory-derived thermal performance curves were integrated with fine-scale field microclimate data to estimate realised performance under natural thermal environments, following the framework proposed by Vasseur et al. (2014) (DOI: 10.1098/rspb.2013.2612).
  • This approach combines experimentally derived thermal performance with the probability distribution of temperatures experienced in the field, providing a more realistic estimate of organismal performance than approaches based solely on coarse climatic averages.

Current Research

My PhD combines laboratory experiments with field microclimate measurements to understand how temperature influences insect performance under realistic environmental conditions. I investigate thermal performance across multiple traits and examine physiological responses to thermal extremes.

Future Research

I aim to develop integrated thermal ecology models that combine laboratory physiology with fine-scale environmental heterogeneity to improve predictions of species responses to climate change and extreme weather events.
